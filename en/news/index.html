21 October 2026Nature

Mapping the insula: how the brain “feels” the body

Neuroscientists studied the insula, a region deep in the brain, in 87 patients who had electrodes implanted for medical reasons. Weak electrical pulses made people feel heat, tingling, numbness and even nausea. The insula turned out to have three zones: the back handles sensations in specific body parts, the middle handles signals from internal organs, and the front links them to emotion and thinking.

21 October 2026Nature

What the Denisovans looked like: new skulls from northern China

Denisovans are mysterious ancient human relatives known for a long time mostly from DNA. Chinese researchers studied skull fragments, teeth and jaws from the Xujiayao site in Hebei province, roughly 140,000–200,000 years old. Ancient proteins and bone shape point to Denisovans, giving the group a recognisable “face” for the first time.

21 October 2026Nature

Plastic that breaks down even on the ocean floor

Compostable plastics degrade poorly at sea, where the right microbes are scarce. Researchers embedded enzymes into four kinds of polyester plastic so that it breaks down from within. In coastal water a PBSA film with just 0.1% enzyme vanished completely in a month, and at a depth of 757 metres samples almost fully degraded within five months.

Source: Nature ↗

21 October 2026Phys.org

A single particle of antimatter trapped for months

Physicists at Northwestern University (USA) held a single positron — an anti-electron — in a magnetic trap at 0.3 kelvin for months and cooled it to its quantum ground state. This paves the way for an ultra-precise measurement of the positron's magnetic moment: any tiny difference from the electron's would point to physics beyond the Standard Model. The work appeared in Physical Review Letters.

20 October 2026Science

Ebola in Congo: betting on the “Tumaini” vaccine trial

The Democratic Republic of the Congo is facing its largest Ebola outbreak ever, caused by the Bundibugyo strain: by 11 October more than 9,000 cases and about 4,400 deaths had been confirmed. A trial called Tumaini (“hope” in Swahili) is being prepared: it will first test the Ervebo vaccine, made against a different virus species, and two new vaccines from Oxford and Moderna may be added later.

19 October 2026Euclid Consortium

The Euclid telescope accidentally found its first comet

ESA's Euclid space telescope was built to map dark matter and deliberately avoids parts of the sky crowded with comets and asteroids. Even so, University of Helsinki PhD student Mikko Pöntinen spotted the trail of a new comet in images taken on 16–24 September. Named C/2026 S1 (Euclid), it is a long-period comet on an orbit nearly perpendicular to the planets' plane, taking thousands of years per lap.

16 October 2026University of Warwick

The first truly green star has been found

Astronomers at the University of Warwick ran white-dwarf spectra through a model of human colour vision to see how these stars would look to our eyes. The white dwarf WD J1330+6435 turned out yellow-green: sodium from the debris of its destroyed planetary system entered its atmosphere and absorbs orange light. Other white dwarfs look pink, purple and cyan.

12 October 2026Phys.org

Helium from the Universe's first five minutes, measured most precisely yet

Almost all the helium in the Universe formed in the first minutes after the Big Bang. Scientists studied 15 “pristine” dwarf galaxies with the Large Binocular Telescope and measured the share of this primordial helium to about 0.5% — three times more precisely than before. The result agrees with cosmic microwave background data: about 90% of the Universe's helium is primordial, the rest was made by stars.

7 October 2026NobelPrize.org

Nobel Prize in Chemistry: why life chose one “hand”

The prize is shared by Henri Kagan (France) and Kenso Soai (Japan) “for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis”. Molecules such as amino acids come in two mirror-image forms, yet living organisms use only one. In 1986 Kagan found a way to get an excess of one form, and in 2003 Soai achieved a reaction that produces only one. This matters for making medicines.

6 October 2026NobelPrize.org

Nobel Prize in Physics: neutrinos caught in Antarctic ice

The prize goes to Francis Halzen (University of Wisconsin–Madison, USA) for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin. IceCube is a cubic kilometre of ice at the South Pole fitted with light sensors that catch the rare flashes from neutrino collisions. Halzen first presented the idea in 1988.

Source: NobelPrize.org ↗

5 October 2026NobelPrize.org

Nobel Prize in Medicine: nerve cells controlled by light

The prize is shared by Karl Deisseroth (USA), Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel (Germany) “for their discoveries concerning light-gated ion channels and optogenetics”. Hegemann and Nagel found the protein channelrhodopsin in a single-celled alga, and Deisseroth turned it into a light-controlled switch for nerve cells. Optogenetics shows how neurons shape memories, feelings and behaviour in the living brain.
