# №02. Journeys to the Moon

**Speaker:** Vladimir Surdin, PhD in Physics and Mathematics, astronomer, science communicator, Sternberg Astronomical Institute, Moscow State University
**Date:** 18 February 2026, 19:20 · Letovo School, lecture hall · A Letovo Wednesday, organised with the support of the Natural Sciences Department's science salon

## [00:00] Introduction: our nearest celestial body

**Speaker:** Good evening! Today we'll talk about the Moon. The topic seems simple: there it is, hanging in the sky, everyone has seen it. But think about it — it's the only celestial body other than Earth that a human has visited. And for more than fifty years no one has gone back. Why? And why go back now? That's what we'll talk about.

[Slide: a photograph of the Moon taken through a telescope]

Let's start with some numbers worth remembering. The average distance to the Moon is 384,000 kilometres. Light covers it in just over a second. A spacecraft takes about three days. The Moon's diameter is about 3,500 kilometres — a little more than a quarter of Earth's. Surface gravity is six times weaker than on Earth. There's practically no atmosphere. A day on the Moon lasts almost a month: two weeks of daylight, two weeks of night. During the day the surface heats up above a hundred degrees Celsius; at night it cools to minus one hundred and seventy.

And here's a question for the audience: why do we always see the same side of the Moon?

**Voice from the audience:** Because it rotates on its axis with the same period as it orbits Earth!

**Speaker:** Exactly right. This is called synchronous rotation, and the cause is tidal interaction. Over billions of years Earth has "slowed down" the Moon's rotation so that it keeps one side facing us. And note: the far side is often called the "dark side". That's wrong. It is lit by the Sun just as much as the near side; we simply can't see it from Earth. That's exactly why the first photograph of the far side was such an event.

## [07:00] The age of robots: first steps

[Slide: "1959 — the year of the Moon"]

**Speaker:** The space age began in October 1957 with the first satellite. And immediately — just over a year later — probes were being sent to the Moon. That shows how desirable a target the Moon was.

**Luna 1**, January 1959. The goal was to hit the Moon. It missed — by about six thousand kilometres. But that wasn't a failure: the probe was the first to reach escape velocity, break free of Earth's gravity and become the first artificial planet — it is still orbiting the Sun today.

**Luna 2**, September 1959. A hit. It was the first human-made object to reach another celestial body. It carried metal pennants with the Soviet coat of arms.

**Luna 3**, October 1959. It flew around the Moon and photographed its far side for the first time. Technically it was a miracle: the probe took pictures on photographic film, the film was developed on board, then the image was scanned and transmitted to Earth by radio. The pictures were of poor quality, but for the first time people saw that the far side was completely different: almost no dark "seas", just craters everywhere.

[Slide: the first image of the far side of the Moon]

**Question from the audience:** Why is the far side so different?

**Speaker:** Good question, and it hasn't been fully answered. The seas, or maria, are huge lava fields that once flowed into large impact basins. On the near side the Moon's crust is thinner, and lava rose to the surface more easily. As for why the crust has different thicknesses, there are several hypotheses: perhaps it has to do with how the Moon cooled while close to the hot young Earth. It's one of the puzzles people are still working on.

Next came the soft landing. That's a very hard task: the Moon has no atmosphere, a parachute won't help, and you can only brake with an engine. The first to land softly was the Soviet probe **Luna 9** in February 1966, which transmitted a panorama of the surface. This, by the way, settled a serious scientific debate. Some scientists believed the Moon was covered in a thick layer of dust and that a lander would simply sink into it. It turned out the surface was solid, though covered with a loose layer called regolith. A few months later the American **Surveyor 1** made a soft landing.

## [18:00] The Moon race and the Apollo missions

**Speaker:** Now the main part of the story. In 1961 US President Kennedy announced that before the end of the decade Americans would land on the Moon and return safely. Why? It was politics. The Soviet Union had launched the first satellite and the first human into space — Gagarin — and the US needed to show it could do more.

[Slide: the Saturn V rocket]

For this the Saturn V rocket was built — still one of the most powerful rockets in history. It was 110 metres tall, roughly a thirty-storey building. Its launch mass was almost three thousand tonnes. And it could send about forty-five tonnes to the Moon.

The flight plan was like this. Three astronauts fly to the Moon in the Apollo spacecraft. They enter lunar orbit. Two of them transfer to the lunar module and land on the surface; the third stays in orbit. Then the ascent stage of the module lifts off from the Moon, docks with the spacecraft, and all three fly home. It was a very bold plan: docking in orbit around the Moon, where there's no help to be had.

- **December 1968, Apollo 8** — the first humans to orbit the Moon. That's when the famous "Earthrise" photo was taken — Earth rising over the lunar horizon.
- **July 1969, Apollo 11** — Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed on the Moon.
- Then five more successful landings. Apollo 13 never reached the Moon because of an accident, but its crew returned safely — one of the most dramatic stories in spaceflight.
- **December 1972, Apollo 17** — the last expedition.

The result: twelve people walked on the Moon. They brought back 382 kilograms of lunar soil. The later expeditions took a lunar car with them and drove tens of kilometres.

**Question from the audience:** Did the Soviet Union have its own Moon programme?

**Speaker:** It did. The N1 rocket and a lunar spacecraft were being developed. But the N1 launched four times — and failed all four times. After the American landing the race lost its meaning, and the programme was closed. Soviet cosmonauts never flew to the Moon. But the USSR bet on robotic probes — and achieved outstanding results there.

## [30:00] Soviet robots: lunar rovers and soil samples

[Slide: Lunokhod 1]

**Speaker:** In September 1970 the **Luna 16** probe landed on the Moon, drilled into the soil, took off and delivered a sample to Earth — about a hundred grams. It was a world first: a robot flew to another celestial body by itself and came back. Then came Luna 20 and Luna 24. In total, about three hundred grams. Not much compared to Apollo, but these samples come from places the Americans never visited, and they are very valuable.

In November 1970 **Lunokhod 1** was delivered to the Moon — the world's first planetary rover. It looked like a bathtub on eight wheels with a hinged lid, with solar panels on the inside of the lid. It was driven from Earth by a crew of five: a driver, a navigator, a commander and others. And with a delay: the signal takes about two and a half seconds to get there and back, plus the image transmission was slow. Imagine driving a car while seeing the road with a delay of several seconds, with the picture updating only once every few seconds. The rover worked for almost eleven months. Lunokhod 2 in 1973 covered almost forty kilometres.

And here's something interesting: both rovers carried corner reflectors — made in France. Apollo astronauts left the same kind of reflectors on the Moon. These reflectors still work today.

**Question from the audience:** What are they for?

**Speaker:** A laser pulse is sent to them from Earth, the light is reflected and comes back. We measure the time — and know the distance to the Moon to within millimetres. Thanks to this we know, for example, that the Moon is moving away from Earth by about 3.8 centimetres a year. That's also a consequence of tides. And by the way — this is one way to answer those who doubt that the Americans went to the Moon. The reflectors are there, and any observatory with suitable equipment can aim a laser at them.

## [40:00] Fifty years on the doorstep

[Slide: "Since 1972 people have not flown beyond low Earth orbit"]

**Speaker:** And so 1973 arrives. The Apollo programme is closed, and so is the Soviet lunar programme. The era of orbital stations begins: Salyut, Skylab, Mir, the ISS. People fly, but at an altitude of a few hundred kilometres. If you imagine Earth as a classroom globe, the ISS passes about a centimetre above its surface. And the Moon is about ten metres away, at the other end of the classroom.

To be precise: there was a recent private flight in which a spacecraft climbed to about one and a half thousand kilometres. But that's still right next to Earth — compared to the Moon, it's nothing.

Why is that? There are several reasons.

**The first is money.** The Apollo programme cost about 25 billion dollars at the time. In today's prices that's more than two hundred billion. In the peak years NASA received more than four per cent of the US federal budget. Now it's less than half a per cent.

**The second is purpose.** The race was won. The political task was done. And science? Science, to be honest, can do a lot with robots, and much more cheaply.

**The third is risk.** A flight to the Moon means leaving the magnetosphere, radiation, and no way to return quickly. Every Apollo expedition was a huge risk.

So for fifty years people sat "on the doorstep". And robots flew. That's worth talking about, because a great deal has happened in the last twenty years.

## [48:00] The new Moon race

[Slide: a map of the Moon with the landing sites of different countries]

**Speaker:** Today many countries are heading for the Moon. Let's see who has done what.

**China.** This is probably the most successful lunar programme right now. It's called Chang'e — after the Moon goddess in Chinese mythology. Step by step: first orbiters, then in 2013 a soft landing with the Yutu rover — the "Jade Rabbit". Then, in 2019, something no one had ever done: a soft landing on the far side of the Moon. That required a relay satellite, because there's no direct line of communication with Earth from the far side. In 2020 Chang'e 5 brought almost two kilograms of soil back to Earth — the first sample return since 1976. And in 2024 Chang'e 6, for the first time in history, returned samples from the far side. China has announced plans to land its astronauts on the Moon before 2030 and is building a rocket and spacecraft for it.

**India.** In 2008 the Indian probe Chandrayaan-1, together with an American instrument, found signs of water on the lunar surface. In 2023 Chandrayaan-3 made a soft landing not far from the south pole — closer to the pole than anyone before it. India became the fourth country to make a soft landing.

**Japan.** In 2024 the Japanese probe SLIM landed on the Moon with very high precision — within a hundred metres of its target. It's true that it tipped over "onto its nose" while landing, but it still worked.

**Russia.** In 2023 Luna 25 was launched — Russia's first lunar probe in almost half a century. Unfortunately, a malfunction occurred while it was preparing to land, and it crashed. This shows how hard it is to rebuild a technology after half a century without working on it. Further probes are planned, including an orbiter and a lander.

**Private companies.** This is something completely new. The Israeli private lander Beresheet crashed in 2019. In 2024 an American company landed a private probe on the Moon for the first time in history — although it also fell on its side during landing. In 2025 another private probe landed quite successfully. NASA now buys "delivery" of scientific instruments to the Moon from private companies — as a service.

**The USA.** And the main one — the Artemis programme. In mythology Artemis is Apollo's twin sister, hence the name. The goal is to return people to the Moon, specifically to the south pole. In 2022 an uncrewed Orion spacecraft flew around the Moon and came back. The next step is Artemis II: a flight around the Moon with four astronauts on board. That flight is being prepared right now, in the coming months. If all goes well, people will fly to the Moon again for the first time in more than fifty years. Then comes a landing, though its date has already been pushed back several times.

**Question from the audience:** Why does everyone want to go to the south pole?

**Speaker:** That's the key question, and it brings us to the main point: why humanity needs the Moon.

## [62:00] Why we need the Moon

[Slide: "Why?" — several points]

**Speaker:** Let's go in order, from the most solid reasons to the most debatable.

### 1. Water

The Moon's axis of rotation is almost perpendicular to the plane of its orbit around the Sun. So at the poles there are craters whose floors are *never* lit by the Sun. It's very cold there — according to measurements, down to minus two hundred and forty degrees Celsius. These are some of the coldest places in the Solar System. And in them, as in traps, volatile substances could have accumulated over billions of years, including water ice. It could have been brought by comets or formed when the solar wind interacted with the soil.

In 2009 an American probe deliberately crashed into one of these craters, while another probe analysed the plume it threw up. They found water.

Why is water so important? For an expedition it's drinking water. If you split it by electrolysis, you get oxygen to breathe, and hydrogen and oxygen are components of rocket fuel. If water can be mined on the Moon, you won't have to bring everything from Earth. And lifting a kilogram of cargo from the Moon, where gravity is six times weaker, is much cheaper than lifting it from Earth.

### 2. The science of how Earth and the Moon formed

The Moon is a museum of the Solar System. It has no atmosphere, no water, no plate tectonics, no life. Everything that has fallen on it over billions of years has stayed there. On Earth the traces of ancient events have been erased; on the Moon they have been preserved.

By studying lunar soil, scientists concluded that the Moon most likely formed when the young Earth collided with a body roughly the size of Mars about four and a half billion years ago. The debris gathered in orbit and formed the Moon. This is called the giant-impact hypothesis. Without the Apollo and Luna samples we wouldn't know this.

### 3. Astronomy from the far side

The far side of the Moon is the quietest place in the radio spectrum near Earth. The whole bulk of the Moon shields it from Earth's radio interference. A radio telescope built there could hear what can't be heard on Earth: for example, very low-frequency signals from the early Universe, which also can't pass through Earth's ionosphere.

### 4. A testing ground for deep space

The Moon is close — three days away. Technologies needed for Mars can be tested there: living on another celestial body, radiation protection, working with local resources. If something goes wrong, you can get back from the Moon in a few days. From Mars — you can't.

### 5. Helium-3 — and here I'll be a sceptic

You often hear: "There's helium-3 on the Moon, it's the fuel of the future, it will solve Earth's energy problems." Let's look into it. Helium-3 is a light isotope of helium. It is carried by the solar wind and accumulates in lunar soil. It really can be used in a fusion reactor.

But! First, its concentration is very low: to get a tonne of helium-3, you'd need to process enormous amounts of soil — hundreds of millions of tonnes. Second, and this is the main point, there is no fusion reactor running on helium-3 yet. There isn't even an industrial fusion power plant running on ordinary fusion fuel — deuterium and tritium — and helium-3 requires even higher temperatures. So talking about mining helium-3 now is like selling tickets for a train whose rails haven't been invented yet.

### 6. Prestige and politics

Let's be honest: this is still an important reason. A country that lands people on the Moon demonstrates its technological power. That's why the Moon race today is again largely political — between the US and China.

## [78:00] The challenges awaiting people on the Moon

**Speaker:** Even if we get there, living on the Moon will be very hard.

**Radiation.** The Moon has neither a magnetic field nor an atmosphere. Cosmic radiation and solar flares strike the surface directly. Living modules will probably have to be covered with several metres of regolith or placed in natural caves — the lava tubes that exist on the Moon.

**Dust.** Lunar dust is not Earth dust. On Earth, dust particles roll around and are worn smooth by wind and water. On the Moon none of that happens, so the particles stay sharp, like shards of glass. They become electrically charged, stick to everything, get into mechanisms and scratch spacesuit visors. Apollo astronauts complained that dust got into the cabin; they said it smelled like gunpowder and irritated their eyes and airways. And their expeditions lasted three days at most. What will happen over months is a big question.

**Temperature and night.** A two-week night at minus one hundred and seventy is a challenge for energy supply. Solar panels don't work at night. You need batteries with colossal capacity or nuclear power sources. That's another reason the poles are interesting: there are elevated areas there that are lit by the Sun almost constantly.

**Low gravity.** What one-sixth g will do to human health over months and years, we don't know. There's simply no such data.

## [85:00] Conclusion

[Slide: "Earthrise"]

**Speaker:** Let's sum up. We've gone from the first probe that missed the Moon in 1959 to a human landing ten years later. Then half a century of silence for humans and busy work for robots. And now a new era is beginning: China, the US, India, Japan, private companies and Russia are all heading for the Moon.

What will this give us? Answers to questions about how Earth and the Moon came to be. New technologies: using local resources, closed life support systems, radiation protection. New telescopes. And perhaps the first step towards Mars.

Or perhaps — and this matters too — it will give us a new generation of people who look at the Moon and think not "how beautiful" but "how do we get there and what do we do there". Thank you!

*(Applause.)*

## [88:00] Questions and answers

**Question:** Did the Americans really go to the Moon?

**Speaker:** *(smiles)* I was waiting for that question. Yes, they did. And there's plenty of evidence; I'll name just a few pieces. First, the lunar soil. The 382 kilograms were studied by scientists around the world, including Soviet scientists: the USSR and the US exchanged samples. Lunar soil can't be faked — it has features that only arise from billions of years of exposure to the solar wind and micrometeorites in a vacuum. Second, the corner reflectors I talked about. Third, modern images: an American probe in lunar orbit has photographed the landing sites, and you can see the descent stages, wheel tracks and paths trodden by the astronauts. Indian and Chinese probes have photographed them too. And finally, fourth: Soviet intelligence watched the flights closely. If there had been the slightest chance to prove a fake, the USSR would have done so on day one. That didn't happen.

**Question:** Why does the flag wave in the photos if there's no wind there?

**Speaker:** Precisely because there's no air! On Earth, a flag that's been shaken settles quickly: the air damps the oscillations. On the Moon there's no air resistance, so once the fabric starts swinging, it keeps moving longer. And so the flag wouldn't hang like a rag, a horizontal rod was sewn into its top edge — which is why it looks unfurled.

**Question:** Will tourists be able to go to the Moon?

**Speaker:** Flying around the Moon — possibly, in the foreseeable future; private companies are discussing such projects. Landing is much harder and more expensive. I wouldn't count on it in the next ten to fifteen years. But you're young — your chances are better than mine.

**Question:** Who owns the Moon? Can you buy a plot there?

**Speaker:** There's the Outer Space Treaty of 1967, signed by almost all spacefaring nations. Under it, the Moon and other celestial bodies cannot be claimed by any state. So the "plots on the Moon" sold online are just pretty certificates with no legal force. But the question of mining resources really is open: can you own what you've mined? Some countries have passed laws allowing their companies to own the resources they extract. This will be one of the main legal problems of the future.

**Question:** What would you advise people who want to study astronomy?

**Speaker:** Study physics and maths — they're the language of astronomy. And programming: today almost all of astronomy is processing huge amounts of data. And don't forget to look at the sky. Buy a small telescope, find the Moon at first quarter — when half the disc is visible. Look at the line dividing day and night: the shadows there are long, and the craters look three-dimensional. It's a sight you never get tired of.

Thank you all!
