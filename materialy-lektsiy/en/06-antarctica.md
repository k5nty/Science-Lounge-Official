# №06. Antarctica: Ice, Life and Research

**Speaker:** Zorigto Namsaraev, PhD in Biology, Head of the Laboratory of Synthetic Biology, Kurchatov Institute National Research Centre

## [00:00] Introduction: a continent-sized laboratory

**Speaker:** Good afternoon! Let's start with a little quiz. Which continent is the coldest?

**Audience:** Antarctica!

**Speaker:** The windiest?

**Audience:** Antarctica again?

**Speaker:** Yes. The highest on average above sea level?

*(Pause.)*

**Voice from the audience:** Asia?

**Speaker:** Many people think so, but no — Antarctica. The average surface height is more than two kilometres, because the continent is covered with a thick layer of ice. And the driest?

**Voice from the audience:** Africa? It has the Sahara.

**Speaker:** No — Antarctica again. In the interior, precipitation is as low as in the driest parts of the Sahara, and in places even lower. It's an ice desert. The coldest, the windiest, the highest, the driest. And at the same time one of the most important laboratories on the planet. Today we'll talk about why.

[Slide: a map of Antarctica with the main research stations]

A few numbers. The area of Antarctica is about 14 million square kilometres. That's almost twice the size of Australia. About 98 per cent of the surface is covered in ice. The average thickness of the ice is about two kilometres, and in places almost five. The volume of ice is about 26 million cubic kilometres. By a common estimate, that's around 70 per cent of all the fresh water on Earth. If all this ice melted, global sea level would rise by almost 60 metres.

## [06:00] How the ice continent formed

**Speaker:** Has Antarctica always been icy? No. And this is one of the most interesting stories in geology.

[Slide: the supercontinent Gondwana — South America, Africa, India, Australia and Antarctica together]

Hundreds of millions of years ago Antarctica was part of a huge southern supercontinent — Gondwana. South America, Africa, India and Australia were joined to it. The climate was much warmer. In Antarctica people find fossilised tree trunks, leaf imprints and coal seams — traces of forests. They find dinosaur remains. And they find fossils of the same ancient plant — Glossopteris — in Antarctica, India, Africa and Australia. This was one of the first pieces of evidence that the continents were once joined.

**Question from the audience:** How could forests grow there if it's dark for half the year?

**Speaker:** Excellent question. Even then Antarctica was close to the pole, and there was a polar night. But the climate was warm, without permanent ice. The plants adapted: in summer, with round-the-clock light, they grew quickly, and in winter they may have shed their leaves, like our deciduous trees. Scientists study these polar forests because there is nothing like them on Earth today.

Then Gondwana began to break apart. The continents drifted away. Australia and South America were the last to separate from Antarctica. Roughly 30–40 million years ago the Drake Passage opened between South America and Antarctica. And something crucial happened: a ring-shaped current formed around Antarctica — the **Antarctic Circumpolar Current**. It's the most powerful ocean current on the planet. It flows in a circle around the continent and, as it were, "fences it off" from the warm waters of lower latitudes.

[Slide: diagram of the circumpolar current]

At the same time, the carbon dioxide content of the atmosphere was falling. These two causes — isolation by the current and a weaker greenhouse effect — led to Antarctica becoming covered by an ice sheet about 34 million years ago. Since then the ice has advanced and retreated a little at times, but overall the continent has stayed icy.

This, by the way, is an important lesson for understanding climate: the arrangement of land and ocean and the amount of CO₂ in the atmosphere can radically change a planet's climate.

## [16:00] Ice as an archive: a time machine kilometres long

**Speaker:** Now — why is Antarctic ice so important for science?

[Slide: an ice core — a cylinder of ice with bubbles]

In the interior of Antarctica, snow almost never melts. Every year a new layer falls and presses down on the older ones. The snow compacts and turns into ice. Tiny air bubbles are trapped inside — samples of the atmosphere from the time that snow fell.

If you drill into the ice sheet and pull out a cylinder of ice — it's called a *core* — the deeper you go, the older the ice. It's like a book where each page is one year, and deep down there are hundreds of thousands of years.

What can be learned from a core:
- **Past temperatures.** The ratio of different oxygen and hydrogen isotopes in the water depends on the temperature at which the snow fell. It's a natural thermometer.
- **The composition of the atmosphere.** In the bubbles you can measure how much carbon dioxide and methane there was.
- **Dust and volcanic ash.** You can find traces of major eruptions and understand how windy and dry the climate was.

A famous result was obtained at Russia's Vostok station. The ice was drilled to a depth of more than three and a half kilometres, and the core covers about 400,000 years. Later, a European project on another dome in Antarctica obtained a core covering about 800,000 years. And work is now under way to retrieve ice older than a million years.

[Slide: graph — temperature and CO₂ over 800,000 years; the two curves move almost in step]

Look at this graph. There are two curves: temperature and carbon dioxide concentration. See how they move almost together? Ice ages — cold, with little CO₂. Interglacials — warm, with more CO₂. Over 800,000 years, carbon dioxide concentration varied from about 180 to 300 parts per million.

And now, mind you, it's more than 420. That's higher than at any time recorded in the Antarctic ice. So Antarctic ice is one of the main pieces of evidence that today's changes in the atmosphere go beyond natural fluctuations.

## [26:00] Life on the edge of the possible

**Speaker:** Now let's talk about life. It seems impossible to live in such a place. But life finds a way.

### An ocean full of life

[Slide: krill, penguins, whales, seals]

Here's a paradox: Antarctica's land is almost lifeless, but the ocean around it is one of the richest in life on the planet. Cold water dissolves more oxygen and carbon dioxide, and deep currents bring nutrients up to the surface. In summer, with round-the-clock light, microscopic algae multiply rapidly.

They're eaten by **Antarctic krill** — a small crustacean a few centimetres long. But there are incredibly many of them: the total mass of krill in the Southern Ocean is estimated at hundreds of millions of tonnes. It may be one of the most abundant animal species on Earth by biomass. The whole food chain rests on krill: fish, penguins, seals and whales. The blue whale, the largest animal in Earth's history, feeds mainly on krill.

**Emperor penguins** are a story of their own. They lay their eggs in the depths of the Antarctic winter, on sea ice. The male holds the egg on his feet, covered by a fold of skin, for about two months, eating almost nothing, in temperatures below minus forty and in strong winds. The penguins huddle in tight groups and constantly move around: those who are cold on the outside work their way in, and those who have warmed up move out to the edge.

### Fish with "transparent" blood

And here's my favourite example. Antarctic waters are home to **icefish**. Their blood has no haemoglobin — the protein that carries oxygen in our blood and makes it red. Their blood is almost transparent. How do they survive? Cold water contains a lot of oxygen, and it is carried simply dissolved in the plasma. They also have large hearts and a lot of blood.

And many Antarctic fish have special **antifreeze proteins** in their blood. The sea water there can drop below zero — to about minus two degrees Celsius, because salt water freezes at a lower temperature. The blood of an ordinary fish would start to freeze at that temperature. Antifreeze proteins stick to tiny ice crystals and stop them from growing.

**Question from the audience:** Can those proteins be used?

**Speaker:** Yes, and they're being studied. For example, for storing biological samples, cells and tissues at low temperatures, and in the food industry — so that ice cream doesn't turn "crystalline". It's one example of how studying extreme life leads to practical technologies.

### Land: mosses, lichens and tiny animals

On land, Antarctica mainly has mosses and lichens. There are only two species of flowering plants, and they're found only on the Antarctic Peninsula, where the climate is milder. The largest animal living permanently on land is a wingless midge a few millimetres long. There are also mites, springtails, nematodes and tardigrades.

### Microorganisms: life inside a rock

[Slide: a cross-section of sandstone — a thin green band just below the surface]

But the real survival champions are **microorganisms**. Antarctica has the so-called Dry Valleys — places with almost no snow or ice, because strong winds blow the snow away and there's very little moisture. They're among the driest and coldest places on Earth. They're often compared to Mars.

And there, if you split a rock — sandstone, for example — just below the surface, a few millimetres deep, you can see a thin coloured band. It's a community of microorganisms — algae, fungi, bacteria and lichens — living *inside* the rock. Why inside? The rock protects them from wind and ultraviolet light and holds a little moisture, while a thin layer of mineral lets through enough light for photosynthesis. These organisms are called cryptoendoliths — "hidden inside stone". They grow incredibly slowly; some communities may take thousands of years to grow.

There are microbial communities on the bottoms of lakes covered by permanent ice many metres thick. There are microbes in salty brines beneath glaciers. And in subglacial lakes, cut off from the surface by kilometres of ice, traces of life have also been found.

**Question from the audience:** Why study microbes in Antarctica?

**Speaker:** There are at least three reasons.

**The first is the limits of life.** Where does life end? At what temperature, dryness or salinity can a cell still function? Antarctica is a natural laboratory for answering these questions.

**The second is the search for life in space.** If microbes live inside rocks in Antarctica's Dry Valleys, could there be something similar beneath the surface of Mars? If there is life in subglacial lakes, could there be life in the oceans beneath the icy crusts of moons of Jupiter and Saturn, such as Europa and Enceladus?

**The third is biotechnology.** Microorganisms that live in the cold have enzymes that work at low temperatures. Such enzymes can be useful in industry: for example, in washing powders that clean well in cold water, which saves the energy needed for heating. Or in medicine and the food industry. Nature has been "developing" these molecules for millions of years, and we are learning to find and use them.

## [48:00] Discovery: the sixth continent

[Slide: the sloops Vostok and Mirny]

**Speaker:** Now for some history. People long suspected that there was a large land in the south. Ancient geographers argued that the Southern Hemisphere must contain land "balancing" the northern continents. Old maps showed a huge "Unknown Southern Land".

In 1772–1775 the British navigator James Cook crossed the Antarctic Circle for the first time. He sailed among the ice but never reached the continent itself. And he concluded that if there was land, it was forever locked in ice and useless to people.

In 1819 a Russian expedition left Kronstadt on two sloops — the Vostok, commanded by Fabian Gottlieb von Bellingshausen, and the Mirny, commanded by Mikhail Lazarev. On 28 January 1820 the expedition approached an ice barrier — an ice shelf off the coast of Antarctica. This date is considered the day Antarctica was discovered.

To be fair: at almost the same time, in the same year 1820, British and American sailors saw the coast of the Antarctic Peninsula, and historians still argue about who was first. But the Bellingshausen and Lazarev expedition was the first purpose-built scientific expedition to sail around Antarctica, map many features and prove that there is a large land surrounded by ice in the high southern latitudes.

The names of its ships remain on the map: the Russian stations Vostok and Mirny are named after them.

## [56:00] The race to the South Pole

[Slide: two routes — Amundsen's and Scott's]

**Speaker:** By the beginning of the 20th century almost the whole Earth had been explored. The poles remained. In 1910–1911 two expeditions set out for the South Pole at almost the same time: a Norwegian one led by Roald Amundsen and a British one led by Robert Scott.

Their approaches were very different.

**Amundsen** bet on dog sledges, skis and the experience of Arctic peoples. He spent a long time studying how the Inuit live and travel: fur clothing, dogs, skis. He carefully planned food depots along the route and marked them so they could be found even in poor visibility. On 14 December 1911 five Norwegians reached the South Pole. And they all came back.

**Scott** used motor sledges, which soon broke down; Manchurian ponies, which coped badly with the frost; and in the end the hard labour of men pulling the sledges themselves. On 17 January 1912 Scott's party of five reached the pole — and saw the Norwegian tent and flag. On the way back all five died: of cold, hunger and exhaustion. Their last tent was only about twenty kilometres from a large food depot. Scott's diary, found later, became one of the best-known records of courage.

**Question from the audience:** Why did Amundsen win? Was he just lucky?

**Speaker:** Luck played a part too — on Scott's return journey, for example, the weather was worse than usual. But the main factors were preparation and choosing the right technology for the specific environment. Amundsen learned from people who had lived in the ice for centuries. That lesson matters today too: in extreme conditions it's not the bravest who wins but the best prepared. Though courage is needed as well, of course.

## [64:00] How Antarctica became a continent of science

[Slide: "The Antarctic Treaty, 1959"]

**Speaker:** After the age of heroic expeditions came the scientific age. The turning point was the International Geophysical Year of 1957–1958. Many countries built research stations in Antarctica. The Soviet Union set up Mirny station on the coast and Vostok station deep in the interior, near the South Geomagnetic Pole. The US built a station right at the geographic South Pole.

And in 1959 the **Antarctic Treaty** was signed. It's a remarkable document, especially given that the Cold War was under way. It was signed by twelve countries, including the USSR and the US. Its main provisions:
- Antarctica is used **for peaceful purposes only** — no military bases, no weapons testing;
- **freedom of scientific research** and the exchange of scientific data;
- states' territorial claims are "frozen" — no one can expand them.

Later, in 1991, a protocol on environmental protection was adopted that bans all mineral extraction except for scientific research. Antarctica is the only continent dedicated entirely to science and peaceful cooperation.

## [70:00] Research today

**Speaker:** What is studied in Antarctica today? A great deal — I'll name a few areas.

**Climate and ice.** How is the ice sheet changing? West Antarctica is losing ice, and some glaciers — such as the huge Thwaites Glacier — particularly worry scientists, because their retreat could speed up sea-level rise. Ice thickness is measured from satellites and aircraft, the ice is drilled, and instruments are placed on the ice itself.

**The atmosphere.** It was in Antarctica in the 1980s that the **ozone hole** was discovered — a sharp thinning of the ozone layer over the continent in spring. The cause turned out to be substances used in refrigerators and aerosol cans — CFCs. In 1987 the Montreal Protocol was signed, banning these substances. And now the ozone layer is slowly recovering. This is perhaps the best example of how science done in Antarctica has changed life for the whole planet.

**Subglacial lakes.** Beneath the ice sheet there are hundreds of lakes of liquid water. How does the water stay liquid? The enormous thickness of ice acts like a blanket, and the Earth's heat warms it slightly from below. On top of that, under high pressure water freezes at a lower temperature. The largest of these lakes is **Lake Vostok**, about 250 kilometres long, under almost four kilometres of ice. In 2012 Russian researchers drilled through the ice and reached the surface of the lake. It was a technically very difficult task, and it was important not to contaminate a lake that had been isolated from the surface, possibly for millions of years.

**Astronomy and physics.** The dry, cold, thin air at high-altitude stations is ideal for certain telescopes. And at the South Pole, a neutrino detector is frozen into the ice — about a cubic kilometre of ice "studded" with sensors. It catches rare flashes of light produced when neutrinos from space collide with molecules of ice.

**Meteorites.** Antarctica is the best place on Earth to look for meteorites. They fall everywhere, but elsewhere they're hard to tell apart from ordinary rocks, while on white ice they stand out immediately. Moreover, the movement of the ice carries meteorites to certain areas where they are especially plentiful. More than half of all meteorites ever found on Earth come from Antarctica. Among them are even pieces of Mars and the Moon.

## [80:00] How polar explorers live and work

[Slide: Vostok station in winter]

**Speaker:** And now about the people. How do they get there, and how do they live?

### Getting there

Getting to Antarctica isn't easy. The main ways are:
- **Research vessels** capable of sailing through ice. The voyage takes weeks. The ships bring fuel, food, equipment and people to the stations.
- **Aircraft.** There are ice runways where large transport planes land, flying in from South Africa, South America, Australia or New Zealand. From there, smaller planes on skis.
- **Overland traverses.** To supply stations deep in the interior, such as Vostok, convoys of tractors pulling sledges set out from the coast. They have to cover more than a thousand kilometres over snow and ice, climbing to more than three and a half kilometres above sea level. Such a traverse takes weeks.

### Life at a station

Polar workers are divided into summer staff, who work only in summer, and winterers, who stay for a year. In winter a station is run by a small team: a few dozen people at some stations, and only about ten to fifteen at Vostok.

A station is a small self-sufficient town. Electricity comes from diesel generators, so fuel is the most precious resource. Water is obtained by melting snow. Food is delivered once a year, for the whole winter. Communication is via satellite.

Now imagine Vostok station. It's almost three and a half kilometres above sea level, and because of how the atmosphere behaves near the pole, the air there is even thinner than at the same altitude elsewhere. There isn't enough oxygen. For the first days after arrival, people sleep badly, have headaches, and find even climbing stairs hard. It was at Vostok, in 1983, that the lowest air temperature ever recorded at the Earth's surface was measured — minus 89.2 degrees Celsius.

**Question from the audience:** What happens if someone gets ill in winter?

**Speaker:** Every station has a doctor. But in winter evacuation is often impossible: planes don't fly during the polar night and in severe frost. So the doctor has to be able to do a great deal — from treating teeth to performing operations. That's why the medical selection of polar workers is so strict.

### Psychology

The polar night lasts for months. The same people around you, monotonous surroundings, separation from family. It's very similar to spaceflight — and it's no coincidence that research is carried out at Antarctic stations to help prepare for long space missions: how people sleep, how their mood changes, how a team functions in isolation.

What helps polar workers? A clear daily routine. Meaningful work — everyone understands why they're there. Traditions and celebrations: for example, midwinter is a big celebration at many stations. And humour — they say you can't get by in Antarctica without it.

## [90:00] Conclusion

[Slide: the aurora over a station]

**Speaker:** Let's sum up.

Antarctica is a continent that was once covered in forests and then, because of continental drift and changes in the composition of the atmosphere, was clad in ice kilometres thick.

Its ice is an archive of Earth's climate over hundreds of thousands of years, and that archive tells us that today's changes in the atmosphere are unusual.

Its life — from krill and penguins to microbes inside rocks — shows how adaptable life is and hints at where to look for it beyond Earth.

Its history is a story of human courage: from the Bellingshausen and Lazarev expedition to Scott's tragedy and today's winterers.

And finally, Antarctica is an example of how countries that disagree on many things managed to agree to dedicate an entire continent to science and peace. And that treaty has been working for more than sixty years.

Thank you!

## [93:00] Questions and answers

**Question:** Can you visit Antarctica as a tourist?

**Speaker:** Yes, tourist cruises go mainly to the Antarctic Peninsula — the "warmest" and most accessible part. It's expensive, and there are strict rules for tourists: you can't go close to the animals, you can't leave anything behind or take anything away, not even a pebble. But the inland stations can only be reached as part of an expedition.

**Question:** How do you become a polar explorer?

**Speaker:** Polar expeditions need all kinds of specialists: scientists — glaciologists, meteorologists, biologists, geophysicists — and also doctors, cooks, mechanics, tractor drivers, electricians and radio operators. You need good health, a profession and a readiness for hardship. Many scientists first get to Antarctica as students or graduate students, as part of research teams.

**Question:** If the ice melts, which cities will suffer?

**Speaker:** Even with very strong warming, the complete melting of Antarctica would take many centuries or millennia. But even a sea-level rise of tens of centimetres this century is already a serious problem for coastal cities and low-lying islands. Exactly how much Antarctica will contribute is one of the main open questions in climate science, and it's exactly what Antarctic researchers are working on now.

**Question:** Are there polar bears in Antarctica?

**Speaker:** *(smiles)* No! Polar bears live in the Arctic, in the north. Penguins live in the Southern Hemisphere. So they'll never meet in the wild. By the way, the word "Antarctica" itself means "opposite the Arctic".

Thank you all!
