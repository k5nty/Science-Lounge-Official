# №01. Interplanetary Flights and Their Medical and Biological Challenges

**Speaker:** Andrey Ratushny, PhD in Biology, Senior Researcher at the Institute of Biomedical Problems of the Russian Academy of Sciences (IBMP RAS), Associate Professor at the Faculty of Space Research, Moscow State University

## [00:00] Introduction: why would a biologist care about space?

**Speaker:** Good afternoon! Thank you for coming on a Friday evening, when you could have gone home already. The announcement said this lecture would interest astronomers, biologists, physicists and chemists. That wasn't just politeness: space medicine is one of the most interdisciplinary sciences there is. To send a person to Mars, a physicist has to calculate the trajectory and the radiation shielding, a chemist has to work out how to produce oxygen and water, and a biologist and a doctor have to understand what will happen to the body over one and a half to three years.

Let's start with a simple question. Raise your hand if you think the main problem with a flight to Mars is the rocket.

*(About half the room raises their hands.)*

**Speaker:** Good. And who thinks the main problem is the human?

*(A few hands go up.)*

**Speaker:** By the end of the lecture, I hope there will be more hands for the second answer. A rocket that can reach Mars can be built — robots have been flying there since the 1960s. But getting a person there healthy, able to work and of sound mind, and then bringing them back, is a problem humanity has not yet solved.

[Slide: "What is an interplanetary flight?" — diagram of the orbits of Earth and Mars]

First, a sense of scale. The International Space Station flies at an altitude of about 400 kilometres. The Moon is about 384,000 kilometres away — a thousand times farther. Mars, depending on where the planets are, is between 55 and 400 *million* kilometres away. A one-way trip on an economical trajectory takes about six to nine months. And you can't leave whenever you like: Earth and Mars line up conveniently roughly once every 26 months. So a typical expedition looks like this: six to nine months there, about a year and a half on Mars waiting for the next "window", and the same trip back. In total, about two and a half to three years.

For comparison: the record for continuous time in space is 437 days. It was set by Russian physician-cosmonaut Valeri Polyakov aboard the Mir station in 1994–1995. Note that he was a doctor. He deliberately experimented on himself to prove that a person can endure a flight as long as the trip to Mars. But even that record is less than half of a full Mars expedition.

## [08:00] What is the Institute of Biomedical Problems?

**Speaker:** A few words about where I work, because it explains where the knowledge I'll be talking about comes from. The Institute of Biomedical Problems, IBMP, was founded in 1963 as the leading institution for space medicine. It takes part in the medical selection of Russian cosmonauts, in their medical monitoring during flight and in their recovery after landing. We also run ground experiments that simulate different factors of spaceflight — we'll come back to those.

Space medicine answers three questions:

1. **What happens** to the body in space?
2. **How can it be prevented** or compensated for?
3. **How do we help a person recover** after returning?

And for each factor of spaceflight — weightlessness, radiation, isolation — the answers are different.

[Slide: "Five main hazards" — weightlessness, radiation, isolation and distance, closed environment, distance from Earth]

NASA, by the way, describes the same hazards in a similar way, and it makes a good plan for our lecture. Let's go through them in order.

## [12:00] Hazard one: weightlessness

**Speaker:** Let's start with the best-known factor. Our bodies evolved over millions of years at a gravity of 1 g. Every system — bones, muscles, heart, the vestibular apparatus — "knows" about gravity and is tuned to it. What happens when gravity is taken away?

### Fluid shift

The first thing cosmonauts notice, literally in the first few hours, is that their faces become puffy and their legs get thinner. This is called "moon face and bird legs".

[Slide: a photo of a cosmonaut before and after reaching orbit]

On Earth, gravity pulls blood and tissue fluid downwards, and we have a whole system — valves in the veins, vessel tone, the work of the leg muscles — that helps return fluid to the heart. In weightlessness this system keeps working, but gravity is gone. As a result, about two litres of fluid move from the lower body to the upper body — into the chest and head.

The body interprets this as excess fluid: "There seems to be too much blood". Receptors in the heart and large vessels send a signal, the kidneys start excreting more water, and within a few days the total volume of blood plasma drops by 10–15 per cent. In space this is fine — the person has adapted. The problems begin on return: gravity pulls the blood down again, but there is less of it, and the vessels have forgotten how to constrict. You stand up — and you faint. This is called orthostatic intolerance.

**Question from the audience:** So is that why cosmonauts are carried out in people's arms after landing?

**Speaker:** Partly, yes. They are carried out and seated in chairs not because they can't take a single step, but because in the first hours the risk of fainting or losing balance is very high, and there's no point taking chances. Several factors are at work here: circulation, muscles and the vestibular system. Let's look at those too.

### Bones

Bone is living tissue. Two processes are constantly going on in it: some cells, osteoclasts, break down bone, and others, osteoblasts, build new bone. The balance between them depends, among other things, on load. A bone that bears weight gets stronger; a bone that isn't loaded gets thinner.

In weightlessness, the bones of the legs, pelvis and spine carry almost no load. The result is a loss of bone mineral density of about 1–1.5 per cent *per month* in the areas most heavily loaded on Earth, such as the neck of the femur. For comparison: an elderly person with osteoporosis loses about 1 per cent per *year*. So in one month a cosmonaut's bones age by a year.

[Slide: graph of bone loss versus flight duration]

And the calcium from the bones doesn't just disappear: it enters the blood and is excreted by the kidneys. That creates another risk — kidney stones. Imagine renal colic halfway to Mars, where the nearest hospital is a hundred million kilometres away.

And the worst part: unlike many other changes, bone recovers slowly after a flight. Some studies show that even a year after a six-month flight, bone density has not fully returned to its original level.

### Muscles

Muscles tell a similar story. The so-called antigravity muscles — the ones that keep us upright: the calves, thighs and back — suffer most. Without load they lose mass and strength. Without countermeasures, you can lose 10–20 per cent of the volume of some leg muscles in six months, and strength drops even more.

The heart is a muscle too. In weightlessness it doesn't have to "pump blood uphill" to the head, and it also loses some of its fitness.

### The vestibular system

In our inner ear there is the otolith organ — tiny crystals of calcium carbonate, otoliths, resting on sensitive hair cells. When you tilt your head, the crystals shift under gravity, and the brain understands which way is up and which way is down.

In weightlessness the otoliths don't "press" on anything — there's no signal about which way is down. The eyes say one thing, the inner ear says another, and the brain tries to reconcile them. About half of people develop space motion sickness in the first days of a flight: nausea, dizziness, headache. After a few days the brain readjusts and starts trusting vision more. And after returning to Earth, it all happens again in reverse.

Now imagine Mars. Seven months of weightlessness, then landing on a planet with a gravity of 0.38 g, and the crew has to start working immediately: leave the spacecraft, set up equipment. No one will carry them out and sit them in a chair. This is one of the key problems.

### Eyes

There is also a relatively new problem that was only truly noticed during long flights on the ISS. Some cosmonauts and astronauts experience a decline in vision: the optic disc swells, folds appear in the retina, and the eyeball flattens slightly. This was named SANS — spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome. It is most likely linked to the same fluid shift towards the head, but the mechanism is not fully understood. Some people have to wear glasses after a flight.

## [30:00] How to fight it: countermeasures

**Speaker:** The good news is that we've learned to cope with many of the effects of weightlessness. The Russian system of countermeasures has been developing since the days of the Salyut and Mir stations.

[Slide: the BD-2 treadmill on the ISS]

**Physical exercise.** A cosmonaut on the ISS exercises for about two hours every day. A treadmill — the person is strapped to it with elastic cords that press them against the belt. An exercise bike. Strength machines. This isn't optional fitness; it's part of the work schedule, just like experiments.

**The "Penguin" loading suit.** This is a suit with a system of elastic cords that constantly pull the body along its axis, loading the muscles and bones as if you were standing. It's called that because people in it walk with a slight waddle.

**The "Chibis" vacuum suit.** These are "trousers" in which lower pressure is created around the lower body. The low pressure pulls blood towards the legs — just as gravity does. Before landing, cosmonauts train in the Chibis so their blood vessels remember how to work when blood is redistributed.

**Water and salt supplements.** Before landing, cosmonauts drink a salt solution to increase their blood volume.

**Pharmacology** — for example, drugs to protect bones. This is an area of active research.

**Question from the audience:** Why not just create artificial gravity? Like in the movies — a rotating spaceship.

**Speaker:** Excellent question — it gets asked at every lecture, and rightly so. Physically, it's possible: rotation creates a centrifugal force, and for the person inside it feels like weight. Let's calculate. Centripetal acceleration equals ω² · r — the square of the angular velocity times the radius.

[Slide: the formula a = ω²r]

We know that most people tolerate rotation up to about 2 revolutions per minute; at faster speeds, any head movement causes severe motion sickness because of the Coriolis effect. Two revolutions per minute is an angular velocity of about 0.21 radians per second. To get 1 g, that is 9.8 m/s², you need a radius of 9.8 / 0.044 — about 220 metres. That's a spacecraft almost the length of a football pitch, and it also has to rotate. You could, of course, make two modules on a tether, which is easier, but it's still a huge engineering challenge.

There is a compromise: a short-radius centrifuge right on board. A person lies in it for some time each day and gets "loaded". We also do such research on the ground. But how much gravity is needed, and for how many hours a day, to keep the body from deteriorating is still an open question. We don't even know for sure whether Mars's 0.38 g is enough to prevent bone loss. You can't test that on Earth — here it's always 1 g.

## [42:00] How to study weightlessness without going to space

**Speaker:** Only a handful of people get to go to space, and research needs hundreds of people. How can the effects of weightlessness be studied on Earth? There are several ways.

[Slide: "Ground-based models of weightlessness"]

**Head-down bed rest.** It sounds scary, but the idea is simple: a person lies in a bed tilted head-down by 6 degrees for several weeks or even months. The tilt causes the same shift of fluid towards the head, and lying down all the time removes the load on bones and muscles. This is the "gold standard" for simulation.

**"Dry" immersion** — a Russian invention. A person lies in a bath of water but is separated from the water by a film. They are effectively suspended; the load is removed from the body almost completely and very evenly. Within a few days the effects resemble those of weightlessness.

**Parabolic flights.** An aircraft flies along a parabola, and for about 20–25 seconds there is real weightlessness inside. It's short, but enough for some experiments.

**Animals.** For example, the Bion biological satellites, which carry mice, insects, plants and microorganisms into space. This makes it possible to study things that can't be studied in humans, such as changes at the level of tissues and cells.

## [48:00] Hazard two: radiation

**Speaker:** Now for the factor that many specialists consider the main obstacle to a flight to Mars: radiation.

On Earth, two things protect us from cosmic radiation: the magnetic field and the atmosphere. The ISS flies inside the magnetosphere, so its crew is partly protected. An interplanetary spacecraft will be outside it.

[Slide: diagram of Earth's magnetosphere and the trajectory to Mars]

There are two main sources of danger.

**Galactic cosmic rays.** These are atomic nuclei — from protons to heavy nuclei such as iron — accelerated to enormous energies somewhere in our Galaxy, for example in supernova explosions. Their flux is relatively constant, but they are very hard to shield against: heavy high-energy nuclei punch through the hull and even create cascades of secondary particles in it. Sometimes thick metal shielding makes things worse, not better.

**Solar particle events.** During powerful solar flares, streams of protons are ejected. They are less energetic and you can shelter from them, but they are unpredictable and can deliver a dangerous dose within a few hours.

Let's look at the numbers. We'll measure in millisieverts — a unit that takes into account the biological effect of radiation.

- On Earth, a person receives about 2–3 millisieverts per *year* from natural background radiation.
- On the ISS — on the order of half a millisievert per *day*.
- The Curiosity rover measured the dose inside its spacecraft during the flight to Mars: about 1.8 millisieverts per day. By its estimates, a round trip would give about 660 millisieverts, not counting time on the surface.

That's a lot. It's comparable to, or even exceeds, the permissible dose for a cosmonaut's entire career.

**Question from the audience:** What will happen to the person? Will they get radiation sickness?

**Speaker:** At such doses spread over years, there won't be acute radiation sickness — for that you need a large dose in a short time. The main risks are long-term: a higher probability of cancer, a possible effect on the cardiovascular system, on the lens of the eye — cataracts. And there is evidence from animal experiments that heavy nuclei may damage the nervous system and affect memory and behaviour. But there's a lot of uncertainty here, because it's hard to reproduce the exact composition of cosmic radiation on Earth.

What can be done? The options are:
- **A shelter** — a small compartment surrounded by stores of water, fuel and food. The hydrogen in water is good at slowing particles down. During a solar flare, the crew hides there.
- **Shorter flight times** — more powerful engines, for example nuclear ones.
- **Forecasting solar activity.**
- **Biological protection** — radioprotectors, substances that reduce cell damage. This is an active area of research, including at our institute.

## [60:00] Hazard three: isolation, confinement and distance

**Speaker:** Now for the most human factor. Imagine: six people, three years, in a space the size of a few rooms. You can't go outside, you can't change your company, you can't call your mum and talk in real time.

[Slide: "Communication delay with Mars: 3 to 22 minutes one way"]

Why can't you call? The signal travels at the speed of light, and depending on where the planets are, it takes between three and twenty-two minutes to reach Mars. You ask a question and get the answer at least six minutes later, and possibly forty minutes later. So there's no "Houston, we have a problem — what should we do?" in real time. The crew has to be autonomous, in both technical and medical situations. Most likely, one crew member should be a doctor, and everyone else should be able to give medical help.

How do you study the psychology of long isolation? Our institute has a ground-based experimental facility — isolated modules where crews can live. The best-known experiment is **Mars-500**. In 2010–2011, six volunteers — three from Russia, one from France, one from Italy and one from China — spent 520 days in isolation. It was a full simulation of a Mars expedition: the "flight" there, the "landing" — three participants even moved into a special module simulating the surface of Mars — and the "flight" back. Communication with "Earth" was delayed by up to twenty minutes, supplies were limited, and there was no way out.

**Question from the audience:** Didn't they fall out with each other?

**Speaker:** *(laughs)* That's the most common question. No, there were no serious conflicts — the crew coped. But there was a lot of interesting stuff. For example, some participants' sleep–wake cycles were disrupted: without the natural change of day and night, the body clock "drifts". By the end of the experiment, some participants had become noticeably less active and spent more time resting — this is called hypokinesia, and it's also a protective response of the mind to monotony. People coped in different ways: some socialised more, others withdrew into themselves.

Now our institute is running a series of international SIRIUS experiments — also isolation for many months, with different goals: for example, simulating work on the surface, operating robots, and interaction in mixed crews.

What we've learned from such experiments:
- **Crew selection** is very important — not only for health, but also for psychological compatibility.
- People need **meaningful work**: when they have interesting tasks, it's much easier for them.
- It's important to keep **contact with Earth**, even with a delay: letters, videos from family.
- There's an interesting effect where motivation drops after the middle of a mission, especially closer to the end. In psychology this is called the "third-quarter effect".

## [72:00] Hazard four: a closed environment and life support

**Speaker:** Now a question for the chemists. A person needs about 0.8–0.9 kilograms of oxygen a day, about 2.5–3 litres of water for drinking and cooking, and about 0.6 kilograms of dry food. Plus water for hygiene. Let's calculate for six people over a thousand days. Oxygen alone — about five tonnes. Water — tens of tonnes. Carrying all of that with you is very expensive. So it has to be recycled.

[Slide: diagram of the ISS life support system]

On the ISS this is already partly done:
- **Oxygen** is produced by electrolysis of water: H₂O is split into hydrogen and oxygen. The Russian system is called "Elektron".
- **Water** is collected from condensation — what we breathe out and evaporate from our skin — and purified. On the American segment, urine is recycled too, and now up to 98 per cent of water can be returned to use.
- **Carbon dioxide** is removed by absorbers.

But food is still entirely brought from Earth. For Mars we need a more closed system — ideally a partly biological one. Plants absorb CO₂, release oxygen and provide food. Lettuce, peas and wheat have already been grown in space, but that's still a very long way from a proper "vegetable garden" that feeds a crew.

**Question from the audience:** What about microbes? In a closed spacecraft they probably multiply, right?

**Speaker:** They certainly do. A human is a walking ecosystem: trillions of microorganisms live in our bodies. In a closed space they colonise everything: surfaces, ventilation systems, panels. On the Mir station there were serious problems with mould and bacteria that damaged materials. Microbiological monitoring is an important part of the work. There is evidence that some bacteria may change in weightlessness: become more resistant to antibiotics, form biofilms more easily. And at the same time, the human immune system weakens during flight. That combination is dangerous.

## [80:00] Hazard five: distance from Earth — medicine without a hospital

**Speaker:** The last one. On the ISS, if something happens, the crew can return to Earth within a few hours. A Soyuz spacecraft is always docked as a lifeboat. On the way to Mars there is no such option. If appendicitis starts, an arm gets broken or a tooth starts to hurt, it will have to be treated on board.

That's why people are developing:
- **Autonomous medical systems** — diagnostics, including ultrasound, which can be performed not by a doctor but by a crew member following a program's prompts.
- **Telemedicine with a delay** — consultations where the answer arrives half an hour later.
- There's a lot of talk now about **artificial intelligence**, which could help with diagnosis when no specialist is available.
- And **prevention**. For example, there's discussion about whether people going on a long expedition should have their appendix removed in advance: this was done with some polar explorers in the past. It's a controversial topic.

## [86:00] Summing up: so when are we going to Mars?

[Slide: "What we know and what we don't"]

**Speaker:** Let's sum up.

**What we can do:** a person can live in weightlessness for more than a year and return. We know how to slow down bone and muscle loss, how to prepare a person for landing, and how to recycle oxygen and water.

**What we can't do yet:**
- reliably protect against galactic cosmic rays;
- fully prevent bone loss;
- guarantee that a crew can work immediately after landing on Mars;
- build a closed life support system that lasts for years;
- treat serious illnesses without the option of evacuation.

How can this be useful here on Earth? In many ways. Methods for fighting bone loss help with osteoporosis. Recovery after long immobility helps bedridden patients. Water purification systems. Telemedicine for remote areas. "Dry" immersion, by the way, is now used in rehabilitation. Space medicine often turns out to be the medicine of the future for everyone.

And one last thing. When people ask me "When will we fly to Mars?", I answer: technically, maybe in a couple of decades. But that requires people who will solve these problems. Physicists, chemists, biologists, doctors, engineers. Perhaps some of them are sitting in this room right now.

Thank you! I'm happy to take questions.

*(Applause.)*

## [90:00] Questions and answers

**Question:** Can you become a cosmonaut if you wear glasses?

**Speaker:** The requirements have relaxed over time. Selection used to be very strict; now some deviations are allowed, especially if they can be corrected. Vision is an important criterion, but not the only one. The main things are general health, endurance, psychological resilience and, of course, education. Almost all modern cosmonauts are engineers, doctors or scientists.

**Question:** Do women cope with space better than men?

**Speaker:** There are differences. For example, according to some data, women more often experience orthostatic intolerance after landing, and men more often experience vision changes. But there isn't much data yet: far fewer women have been to space. We can't say that anyone is "better" overall.

**Question:** What happened with the twins, one of whom was sent to the ISS?

**Speaker:** That's NASA's study of the Kelly brothers: Scott spent almost a year on the ISS, while his twin brother Mark stayed on Earth. It's a rare opportunity to compare people with identical genes. They found changes in gene expression, in the immune system, and interesting changes in the length of telomeres — the end sections of chromosomes. Most of the changes returned to normal after the flight, but some persisted. It's important to understand that this is one person, and you can't draw big conclusions from one person. But as a starting point it's very valuable.

**Question:** Could a person be put into hibernation for the flight?

**Speaker:** Every science-fiction writer's dream! There are animals that hibernate: bears, marmots. Interestingly, a bear loses almost no bone or muscle mass over the winter. If we understood how that works, it could help both cosmonauts and bedridden patients. There is research on artificial hypometabolism — slowing down the metabolism. But we're still very far from human hibernation.

**Question:** How many years do you need to study to work at your institute?

**Speaker:** *(laughs)* You can start as a student. Many people come to us while still at university — biologists, medics, physicists, engineers. If you're interested, study biology and physics, don't give up maths and English. And come and see us.

Thank you again!
