# №04. How Does Life Begin? Does the Embryo Break the Laws of Physics?

## [00:00] Introduction: the most amazing transformation

**Speaker:** Good afternoon! I'll start with a picture.

[Slide: a fertilised human egg under a microscope — a sphere with a transparent coat]

This is a single cell. A fertilised human egg, a zygote. Its diameter is about a tenth of a millimetre. You could see it with the naked eye if you tried really hard — it's the size of a dot made with a sharp pencil. Let's calculate its volume. The radius is about 60 micrometres. The volume of a sphere is four-thirds pi r cubed. That comes to about 0.9 nanolitres. In other words, less than a billionth of a litre.

[Slide: a photograph of a newborn baby]

And this is what it will become about nine months later. Around three kilograms. Trillions of cells — and an adult human will have around 30 trillion. Hundreds of different cell types: neurons, muscle fibres, blood cells, liver cells. And all of this is arranged not randomly but in strict order: the heart on the left, the liver on the right, five fingers on each hand, the eyes in the right place.

The mass has increased roughly three billion times. But the main thing that has grown is *complexity*. An almost uniform sphere has turned into the most complex structure we know.

And this is where any physicist in the room should have a question. Who knows the second law of thermodynamics?

**Voice from the audience:** The entropy of a closed system never decreases!

**Speaker:** Exactly. And in simpler words?

**Voice from the audience:** Everything tends towards disorder.

**Speaker:** There you go! And that's the main question of our lecture. If everything in the world tends towards disorder — a cup breaks rather than putting itself back together, hot tea cools down rather than heating up by itself, a house without repairs falls apart — then how can an embryo exist that goes from simple to complex? Doesn't it break the laws of physics?

Spoiler: it doesn't. But *how* it manages not to break them is one of the most beautiful stories in science.

## [08:00] What entropy really is

[Slide: two boxes of balls — one ordered, one mixed]

**Speaker:** First, let's carefully work out what entropy is, because the word "disorder" is a little misleading here.

Imagine a box divided by a partition. On the left there are a hundred red balls, on the right a hundred blue ones. Let's remove the partition and shake the box. What happens?

**Voice from the audience:** They'll mix.

**Speaker:** Of course. And if we keep shaking — will they ever separate again, red on the left and blue on the right?

**Voice from the audience:** In theory they could, but it's unlikely.

**Speaker:** That's the essence of entropy. No law *forbids* the balls from separating. It's just that there are astronomically more ways to arrange the balls "mixed" than "separated". In the 19th century Ludwig Boltzmann expressed this with a formula: entropy is proportional to the logarithm of the number of ways a given state can be achieved. States that can be achieved in more ways are more probable. The system simply "wanders" at random and almost always ends up in the most probable states.

So the second law is not a law of "striving towards disorder" but a law of *probability*. A system moves from less probable states to more probable ones.

But there's an important word in the statement. Which one?

**Voice from the audience:** "Closed"?

**Speaker:** Yes. Entropy doesn't decrease in a *closed*, or isolated, system — one that exchanges neither matter nor energy with its surroundings. Is an embryo a closed system?

**Voice from the audience:** No. It feeds.

**Speaker:** So we've found the first loophole. Remember it; we'll come back to it.

## [16:00] How non-living things become ordered

**Speaker:** Before talking about living things, let's see whether *non-living* things can become ordered. If not, then life really is something special. If they can, then life may be using the same principles.

### A snowflake

[Slide: a photograph of a snowflake]

Look at a snowflake. Six-armed, symmetrical, amazingly intricate. It forms from water vapour — a gas in which the molecules fly around completely chaotically. Water vapour has high entropy, and an ice crystal has low entropy. So when a snowflake forms, does entropy decrease?

In the snowflake itself — yes. But when water crystallises, heat is released. That heat goes into the surrounding air and increases its entropy. And it increases it by more than the snowflake's entropy decreased. In total — snowflake plus surrounding air — entropy grows. The law holds.

That's the first important principle: **order in one place can be created at the cost of even more disorder somewhere else**.

### Bénard cells

[Slide: video — a thin layer of oil heated from below; hexagonal cells appear on the surface]

Here's a more interesting example. Take a thin layer of liquid, such as oil, and heat it from below. While the heating is weak, heat simply passes upwards from molecule to molecule, and nothing is visible. Then, when the temperature difference becomes large enough, suddenly — snap! — a pattern of hexagonal cells appears on the surface. Inside each cell the liquid rises in the centre and sinks at the edges. Billions upon billions of molecules start moving in a coordinated way.

How is this different from a snowflake? Once formed, a snowflake can lie there for as long as you like — that's *equilibrium* order. Bénard cells exist only while heat flows through the liquid. Turn off the stove and the pattern disappears. It's order that exists thanks to a constant *flow of energy* through the system.

The Belgian physicist Ilya Prigogine — who, incidentally, was born in Moscow — called such structures *dissipative*. "Dissipation" means the scattering of energy. These structures exist by dissipating energy. Prigogine received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1977 for studying such systems.

### The Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction

[Slide: video — concentric and spiral coloured waves spreading in a Petri dish]

And now the most beautiful example. In 1951 the Soviet chemist Boris Belousov discovered a strange reaction: the solution in a flask periodically changed colour — from colourless to yellow and back, like a clock. His paper was rejected: reviewers thought it was impossible, that it contradicted thermodynamics. A chemical reaction is supposed to go in one direction, towards equilibrium, not "swing" back and forth.

Later Anatoly Zhabotinsky continued this research, and it turned out that if you pour this solution into a dish in a thin layer, travelling waves, concentric rings and spirals appear in it. Beautiful — as if it were alive.

Does it contradict thermodynamics? No. The reaction does eventually reach equilibrium once the starting substances run out. But along the way the system can pass through complex, ordered, rhythmic states. As long as there's "fuel", there are patterns.

**Question from the audience:** So Bénard cells and this reaction are a kind of "almost life"?

**Speaker:** Good observation. They're definitely not alive: they don't reproduce, inherit properties or evolve. But they show that *self-organisation* — order arising "by itself" — doesn't contradict physics. It just has to be "fed" with a flow of energy or matter.

## [32:00] Erwin Schrödinger and "negative entropy"

[Slide: the cover of Schrödinger's book "What Is Life?", 1944]

**Speaker:** In 1944 Erwin Schrödinger, one of the founders of quantum mechanics, wrote a small book called "What Is Life?". He asked the question we're discussing now: how does a living organism avoid decay, destruction, "heat death"?

Schrödinger's answer was that an organism "feeds on negative entropy". It sounds mysterious, but the meaning is simple. We don't just eat calories. We eat *ordered* matter — complex molecules of proteins, fats and carbohydrates. Inside us they're broken down into simple ones — carbon dioxide, water, urea. That's a transition from order to disorder. Entropy grows. And thanks to that growth in entropy, we maintain our own order. We "dump" the excess entropy into the environment — as heat we radiate, and as simple molecules we breathe out and excrete.

A person at rest produces about 100 watts of heat — like an old incandescent light bulb. That's our entropy "dump".

**Question from the audience:** What about plants? They don't eat.

**Speaker:** Plants feed on the highest-quality "order" there is — sunlight. The Sun is a hot source with a surface temperature of about 6,000 degrees. Earth radiates heat into space at a temperature of about minus twenty degrees Celsius. Each "hot" photon arriving from the Sun turns into about twenty "cold" photons leaving for space. The entropy of cold radiation is much higher. That difference — between the hot Sun and cold space — is the flow on which all life on Earth depends.

So the answer to the question "does life violate the second law of thermodynamics?" is no. Life is an open system that creates order inside itself by speeding up the growth of disorder outside.

**But** — and this is where real biology begins — that answer is *necessary* but not *sufficient*. Thermodynamics says that order *can* be created. But it doesn't say *what kind* of order will result. Why does a cat give birth to kittens and not to Bénard cells? Why five fingers and not seven? That's no longer just a question of energy, but of *information* and *organisation*.

## [44:00] How an embryo develops

[Slide: stages of development — zygote, cleavage, blastula, gastrula, neurula]

**Speaker:** Now let's look at what actually happens in an embryo. I'll show a general scheme that's similar for very many animals — from frogs to humans.

**Cleavage.** The zygote divides into two cells, then four, eight, sixteen. Interestingly, at this stage the embryo hardly grows: the cells just get smaller and smaller. One big cell is "sliced" into many small ones.

**Blastula.** The result is a ball of cells, often with a cavity inside. There are already hundreds or thousands of cells, but they're still fairly similar to each other.

**Gastrulation.** And here comes perhaps the most dramatic event in the life of any animal. The British biologist Lewis Wolpert once put it this way: the most important moment in your life is not birth, marriage or death, but gastrulation. Some of the cells move inside the ball, and the embryo becomes multi-layered. Three germ layers form: the outer one, which will give rise to the skin and nervous system; the middle one — muscles, bones and blood; and the inner one — the gut and its associated organs. A body axis appears, with a "front" and a "back", a "top" and a "bottom".

**Neurulation.** On the back side a plate forms and rolls up into a tube — the future brain and spinal cord.

And then — organs, limbs and so on.

The question is: how does each cell "know" what to become and where to go?

## [52:00] Determinism or freedom: the experiments of Roux and Driesch

[Slide: diagram of the experiments of Roux (frog) and Driesch (sea urchin)]

**Speaker:** At the end of the 19th century there were two answers to this question.

The first: everything is *predetermined*. The zygote already contains a "plan", like the mechanism of a clock. With each division the cells receive different "parts of the plan", and each cell is like a part that knows its place.

The German embryologist Wilhelm Roux decided to test this in 1888. He took a frog embryo at the two-cell stage and killed one of the cells with a hot needle. The remaining cell developed into *half* an embryo. Conclusion: each cell knows its "half of the plan". A mechanism!

Then, in 1891, his compatriot Hans Driesch did a similar experiment, but with a sea urchin and slightly differently: he didn't kill one cell but *separated* the two cells by shaking them. And each one developed not into half a larva but into a *whole* one — just a small one.

[Slide: two small but complete sea urchin larvae]

Driesch was stunned. How can a "clock part" taken out of the mechanism become a whole clock by itself? So at early stages a cell doesn't know what it will become. Its fate is determined by *where it is* and *what's around it*. The embryo as a whole is able to *regulate* its development.

**Question from the audience:** So why did Roux get half an embryo?

**Speaker:** Because the dead cell didn't disappear; it stayed next to the living one. The living cell "felt" its neighbour and behaved as if it were half of a whole. In Driesch's experiment the cell was on its own and "decided" it was the whole. It's a brilliant example of how important it is *how* an experiment is set up.

By the way, humans have their own "Driesch experiment" — identical twins. That's when an early embryo splits into two parts, and each part develops into a whole person.

Driesch, incidentally, drew a philosophical conclusion from this: since an embryo can't be explained as a machine, it must contain a special "life force", which he called entelechy. Science didn't accept that conclusion. But he asked the right question: how can a system made of many parts "know" what it should be as a whole?

## [62:00] How does a cell know where it is?

**Speaker:** People searched for answers to this question throughout the 20th century. I'll tell you about three big ideas.

### The organiser

In 1924 Hans Spemann and his graduate student Hilde Mangold transplanted a small piece of tissue from one newt embryo to another. And the host embryo developed a *second body axis* — practically a second embryo, a conjoined twin. And it was built mostly from the host's cells! The transplanted piece "told" the neighbouring cells what to become. It was named the *organiser*. Spemann received the Nobel Prize for this in 1935.

This showed that cells communicate. Some cells send signals, and others respond to them.

### Morphogens and positional information

How exactly are signals transmitted? One idea is *morphogens*. These are substances released from one place that spread through the embryo, creating a concentration gradient: high near the source, low far away.

[Slide: Wolpert's "French flag" — a strip of cells, a gradient and three zones: blue, white, red]

That same Wolpert proposed a vivid model — the "French flag". Imagine a strip of cells. A morphogen is released from one end. The cells "measure" its concentration. If the concentration is high, I become "blue". Medium — "white". Low — "red". The result is a three-coloured flag. And if you cut off part of the strip, the gradient rearranges itself and the flag is restored in the right proportions.

In the fruit fly Drosophila, a real morphogen of this kind was found — the protein Bicoid. The mother "places" its messenger RNA at the front end of the egg, and it creates a gradient the embryo uses to determine where the head will be.

### Turing patterns

And now an unexpected character. Alan Turing — the same man who laid the foundations of computer science and broke the Enigma cipher machine. In 1952 he published a paper called "The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis". He showed mathematically that if two substances react with each other and also diffuse at different speeds, then spots, stripes and periodic patterns can arise *by themselves* from an almost uniform mixture.

[Slide: the skin of a leopard, a zebra and a fish; next to them, results of computer simulations]

This is very similar to what we saw in the Belousov reaction. There's now evidence that mechanisms of this kind are involved in forming stripes on the skin of some fish, in the arrangement of hair follicles, and possibly in the formation of fingers.

### Mechanics

And there's another side that people often forget. An embryo isn't just chemistry; it's also *mechanics*. Cells pull on each other, contract, stretch. Tissues bend like sheets of paper. Mechanical stress can itself act as a signal: a cell "feels" that it's being stretched and changes its behaviour. There is a whole research tradition — including in Moscow, at Moscow State University — showing that the shape of an embryo and the mechanical stresses in it are linked into a single system with feedback. Shape creates stress, and stress changes shape.

## [76:00] Why the genome isn't enough

**Speaker:** Many people think: "Well, it's all written in the DNA. A genetic program — and everything develops according to it." Let's test that with some arithmetic.

[Slide: "The human genome: about 3 billion base pairs"]

The human genome has about three billion base pairs. Each pair is one of four "letters", that is, two bits. That makes about six billion bits — roughly 750 megabytes. That's less than one high-quality film.

Now take the brain. It has about 86 billion neurons, and between them there are on the order of a hundred trillion connections, or synapses. If the genome described every connection — "neuron number such-and-such connects to neuron number such-and-such" — it would need many orders of magnitude more information than the whole genome contains.

So the genome is *not a blueprint*. It's more like *a set of rules*. Something like: "If you're a cell, and there's a lot of substance A around you, and you're being stretched — start dividing." And the complex structure emerges from how these rules interact with each other, with physical laws, and with the mechanics and chemistry of the environment. That's self-organisation.

A good analogy is a flock of birds. There's no "lead bird" telling everyone where to fly. Each bird follows simple rules: stay close to your neighbours, don't crash into them, fly where they fly. And the result is the stunning dancing flocks of thousands of starlings.

**Question from the audience:** So what is the "self-consistency" mentioned in the announcement?

**Speaker:** Excellent question. What I mean by it is this. In development there's no single "boss" in charge of everything. There's no "conductor". The parts of the system *agree with each other*. Cells affect the shape of tissue, the shape of tissue affects signals, signals affect cells, and round it goes. And the system finds a stable state in which everything is consistent. That's why an embryo can "correct" mistakes: if something goes wrong, the neighbouring parts adjust. That's what Driesch saw, but it was explained not by a "life force" but by a network of interactions.

## [84:00] The arrow of time in physics and in biology

[Slide: "The arrow of time"]

**Speaker:** Now for the last question from the announcement — the "arrow of time".

In Newtonian mechanics time is *reversible*. If you film billiard balls colliding and play the recording backwards, you can't tell which is the original and which is reversed. The equations work equally well forwards and backwards.

But in the real world we can easily tell the past from the future. A broken cup played backwards puts itself back together — and we immediately know it's a trick. Why? Because of the second law of thermodynamics: entropy increases, and that sets the direction of time. This is the *thermodynamic arrow of time*. It points towards destruction, levelling out, mixing.

Now let's watch a video of an embryo developing.

[Slide: time-lapse video of a zebrafish embryo developing]

If you play it backwards, we immediately know it's reversed. So development has an arrow of time too; it is also irreversible. But at first glance it points the opposite way: towards *greater complexity*.

So does nature have two arrows? One towards disorder, the other towards order?

In fact there's only one arrow. An embryo becomes more complex only because it's part of a larger flow — from the hot Sun, through food, to cold space — in which entropy increases. The biological arrow "rides" on the thermodynamic one, like a surfer riding a wave. A surfer can even move at an angle to the wave, but without the wave they won't go anywhere.

But there's an important difference. A cup breaks *any old way* — differently every time. An embryo develops *reproducibly*: every time you get a mouse, not something random. This is not random complexity but *directed* complexity. And here thermodynamics alone can't explain everything. We need biology: evolution, which over billions of years selected sets of rules that produce a stable result.

The British biologist Conrad Waddington proposed an image I really like. Imagine a hilly landscape with valleys running downhill. A cell is a ball rolling across this landscape. At forks in the valleys the ball "chooses" which way to roll, and in the end it lands in one of the valleys — it has become a nerve cell, a muscle cell or a skin cell. If you nudge the ball a little, it will roll back into its valley. That's the robustness of development.

[Slide: Waddington's epigenetic landscape]

## [92:00] Conclusion

**Speaker:** Let's return to our question: does the embryo break the laws of physics?

**No.** An embryo is an open system. It creates order inside itself while increasing disorder around it: it consumes nutrients and releases heat and simple molecules. In total, the entropy of the Universe increases, as it should.

**But** physics only explains that development is *possible*. *How* exactly one cell turns into an organism is a question of self-organisation: the interaction of genes, chemical signals and mechanical forces that constantly adjust to each other.

And for me, that's the most amazing thing. Not that life "breaks" physics, but that it uses physical laws so inventively that it produces something that seemingly shouldn't be possible. From one cell — you. Every one of you sitting in this room.

Thank you!

*(Applause.)*

## [95:00] Questions and answers

**Question:** Can a whole organism be grown in a test tube?

**Speaker:** Not yet. But there's very interesting work with *organoids* — small organ-like structures grown from stem cells. There are organoids of the gut, the kidney, the retina, even mini "brains". And recently scientists have learned to make *embryo models* from stem cells — structures that go through some early stages of development. This shows how powerful self-organisation is: if you create the right conditions, cells assemble themselves into complex structures. Of course, this also raises serious ethical questions, and scientists are actively discussing them.

**Question:** Why do identical twins have different fingerprints if their genes are the same?

**Speaker:** A perfect example of what we've been talking about! Genes set the *rules*, but not every detail. Fingerprint patterns form during a particular period of development in the womb and depend on lots of small factors: position in the womb, pressure, growth rate, random fluctuations. The rules are the same, but the result differs in its details. It's like running the same Belousov reaction twice — the patterns will be of a similar type, but not identical.

**Question:** Is there randomness in development?

**Speaker:** A great deal. For example, in an individual cell the number of molecules of some proteins is very small, and they're produced in random "bursts". Two identical neighbouring cells can have different amounts of the same protein. What's amazing is not that there's randomness, but that despite it the outcome is so reliable. The system is built so that random fluctuations are damped. And sometimes, on the contrary, randomness is put to use: for example, so that within a group of identical cells some "choose" one fate and others another.

**Question:** Why did you become an embryologist?

**Speaker:** *(smiles)* Because the embryo has everything. Physics, chemistry, maths, genetics, mechanics. And there's the question "how?", which still has no complete answer. That's the most exciting thing a scientist can have — a question without an answer.

Thank you all!
